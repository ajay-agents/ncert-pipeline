Work to assemble charges on a square

Four charges are arranged at the corners of a square ABCD of side d, as shown in Fig. 2.15. How much extra work is needed to do this?

Part (a)

Find the work required to put together this arrangement.

Given
The work done depends only on the final arrangement of the charges, not on how we bring them together.
So we find the work needed for one way of assembling them: bring +q to A first, then the charges -q,+q and -q to B, C and D respectively.
We add up the work in four steps:
Substitute
Bringing charge +q to A, with no charge anywhere else, needs zero work.
Bringing -q to B, with +q already at A, needs (charge at B) × (potential at B due to charge +q at A):
 = -q × q4 π ε0 d = -q24 π ε0 d
Bringing charge +q to C, with +q at A and -q at B, needs (charge at C) × (potential at C due to the charges at A and B):
 = +q +q4 π ε0 d √(2)+-q4 π ε0 d=-q24 π ε0 d1-1√(2)
Bringing -q to D, with +q at A,-q at B, and +q at C, needs (charge at D) × ( tential at D due to the charges at A, B and C):
 = -q +q4 π ε0 d+-q4 π ε0 d √(2)+q4 π ε0 d=-q24 π ε0 d2-1√(2)
Conclusion
Adding the work from steps (i)-(iv) gives the total work required:
=-q24 π ε0 d(0)+(1)+1-1√(2)+2-1√(2)=-q24 π ε0 d(4-√(2))
This depends only on how the charges are arranged, not on the order in which we bring them together — by definition, this is the total electrostatic energy of the charges.
(Students may try calculating the same energy in a different order and check that it comes out the same.)
-q24 π ε0 d(4-√(2))

Part (b)

A charge q0 is brought to the centre E of the square, the four charges being held fixed at its corners. How much extra work is needed to do this?

Conclusion
The extra work necessary to bring a charge q0 to the point E when the four charges are at A, B, C and D is q0 ×  (electrostatic potential at E due to the charges at A, B, C and D ).
The electrostatic potential at E is clearly zero since potential due to A and C is cancelled by that due to B and D.
Hence, no work is required to bring any charge to point E.

Heat released reorienting dipoles in a field

A molecule of a substance has a permanent electric dipole moment of magnitude 10‑29 C m. A mole of this substance is polarised (at low temperature) by applying a strong electrostatic field of magnitude 106 V m‑1. The direction of the field is suddenly changed by an angle of 60°. Estimate the heat released by the substance in aligning its dipoles along the new direction of the field. For simplicity, assume 100\% polarisation of the sample.

Given
The dipole moment of each molecule is  = 10‑29 C m.
One mole of the substance has 6 × 1023 molecules, so the total dipole moment of all the molecules is p = 6 × 1023 × 10‑29 C m  = 6 × 10‑6 C m.
Substitute
Initial potential energy, Ui = -p E cos θ = ‑6 × 10‑6 × 106 cos 0° = ‑6 J.

Change in potential energy  = ‑3 J-(‑6 J) = 3 J.
Conclusion
So there is a loss in potential energy — this must be the energy released by the substance as heat while its dipoles align.

Conductors, insulators and electrostatic safety

A man standing on the ground touches the same line and gets a fatal shock. Why?

Part (a)

A comb run through one's dry hair attracts small bits of paper. Why? What happens if the hair is wet or if it is a rainy day? (Remember, a paper does not conduct electricity.)

Conclusion
This is because the comb gets charged by friction.
The molecules in the paper gets polarised by the charged comb, resulting in a net force of attraction.
If the hair is wet, or if it is rainy day, friction between hair and the comb reduces.
The comb does not get charged and thus it will not attract small bits of paper.
charging by friction polarises the paper's molecules, giving a net attractive force; wet hair reduces friction, so the comb no longer charges and no longer attracts the paper

Part (b)

Ordinary rubber is an insulator. But special rubber tyres of aircraft are made slightly conducting. Why is this necessary?

Conclusion
To let the charge built up by friction flow safely to the ground.
If too much static electricity builds up, it can spark and start a fire.
to conduct away friction-produced charge before it sparks and causes fire

Part (c)

Vehicles carrying inflammable materials usually have metallic ropes touching the ground during motion. Why?

Conclusion
Reason similar to (b).
same reason as (b) - to conduct away friction-produced charge safely to the ground

Part (d)

A bird perches on a bare high power line, and nothing happens to the bird. A man standing on the ground touches the same line and gets a fatal shock. Why?

Conclusion
Current passes only when there is difference in potential.
the bird's two feet are at (nearly) the same potential, so no current flows through it; the man, in contact with the line and the ground, spans a large potential difference

Energy stored when capacitors are connected

What is the electrostatic energy stored by the system?

Part (a)

A 900 pF capacitor is charged by 100 V battery [Fig. 2.31(a)]. How much electrostatic energy is stored by the capacitor?

Conclusion
The charge on the capacitor is
Q = C V = 900 × 10‑12 F × 100 V = 9 × 10‑8 C
The energy stored by the capacitor is
=12CV2=12Q V=12 × 9 × 10‑8 C × 100 V = 4.5 × 10‑6 J
4.5 × 10‑6 J

Part (b)

The capacitor is disconnected from the battery and connected to another 900 pF capacitor [Fig. 2.31(b)]. What is the electrostatic energy stored by the system?

Conclusion
In the steady state, both capacitors have their positive plates at the same potential, and their negative plates at the same potential.
Let this common potential difference be V′, so the charge on each capacitor is Q′ = C V′.
Charge conservation gives Q′=Q2, so V′=V2.
The total energy of the system is
 = 2 × 12 Q′ V′=14Q V = 2.25 × 10‑6 J
So no charge is lost in going from (a) to (b), but the final energy is only half the initial energy.
2.25 × 10‑6 J (half the initial energy; the rest is lost as heat and electromagnetic radiation during the transient)
Where did the energy go?
There is a brief transient period before the system settles into (b).
During this time, a transient current flows from the first capacitor to the second, and energy is lost as heat and electromagnetic radiation.
