# Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance

## Examples

:::example{number="2.1" kind="example" id="ex_2.1" topic="Potential and work done near a point charge"}
#### Example 2.1

:::prompt
(a) Calculate the potential at a point P due to a charge of $4 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{C}$ located 9 cm away.
(b) Hence obtain the work done in bringing a charge of $2 \times 10^{-9} \mathrm{C}$ from infinity to the point P. Does the answer depend on the path along which the charge is brought?
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
Calculate the potential at a point P due to a charge of $4 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{C}$ located 9 cm away.
:::

:::solution
:::formula{label="Key formula"}
$$
\begin{aligned}
V & =\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{Q}{r}=9 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{Nm}^{2} \mathrm{C}^{-2} \times \frac{4 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{C}}{0.09 \mathrm{~m}} \\
& =4 \times 10^{4} \mathrm{~V}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $V=4 \times 10^{4} \mathrm{~V}$
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
Hence obtain the work done in bringing a charge of $2 \times 10^{-9} \mathrm{C}$ from infinity to the point P. Does the answer depend on the path along which the charge is brought?
:::

:::solution
:::formula{label="Key formula"}
$$
\begin{aligned}
W & =q V=2 \times 10^{-9} \mathrm{C} \times 4 \times 10^{4} \mathrm{~V} \\
& =8 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~J}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::
No, the work done does not depend on the path. Any small path can be split into two perpendicular parts: one along r, and one perpendicular to r. The work done along the perpendicular part is zero.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $W=8 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~J}$; no, work done is path independent
:::

:::

:::example{number="2.2" kind="example" id="ex_2.2" topic="Point of zero potential between two charges"}
#### Example 2.2

:::prompt
Two charges $3 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C}$ and $-2 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C}$ are located 15 cm apart. At what point on the line joining the two charges is the electric potential zero? Take the potential at infinity to be zero.
:::

:::figure{src="images/fig_physics-12-2_6.jpg" id="fig_physics-12-2_6"}
FIGURE 2.7
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
Let us take the origin O at the location of the positive charge, with the line joining the two charges as the $x$-axis and the negative charge on the right of the origin (Fig. 2.7).

Let P be the required point on the $x$-axis where the potential is zero, with $x$ its $x$-coordinate. The potentials from the two charges cannot add up to zero for $x<0$, so $x$ must be positive.

:::step{n="1"}
If $x$ lies between O and A , we have
$$
\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{3 \times 10^{-8}}{x \times 10^{-2}}-\frac{2 \times 10^{-8}}{(15-x) \times 10^{-2}}=0
$$
where $x$ is in cm. That is,
$$
\frac{3}{x}-\frac{2}{15-x}=0
$$
which gives $x=9 \mathrm{~cm}$.
:::

:::step{n="2"}
If $x$ lies on the extended line OA, the required condition is
$$
\frac{3}{x}-\frac{2}{x-15}=0
$$
which gives
$$
x=45 \mathrm{~cm}
$$
:::

So the electric potential is zero at 9 cm and at 45 cm from the positive charge, on the side of the negative charge. This uses the convention that potential is zero at infinity.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** zero at 9 cm and at 45 cm from the positive charge, on the side of the negative charge
:::

:::example{number="2.3" kind="example" id="ex_2.3" topic="Signs of potential and work along field lines"}
#### Example 2.3

:::prompt
Figures 2.8 (a) and (b) show the field lines of a positive and negative point charge respectively.
(a) Give the signs of the potential difference $V_{\mathrm{P}}-V_{\mathrm{O}}$; $V_{\mathrm{B}}-V_{\mathrm{A}}$.
(b) Give the sign of the potential energy difference of a small negative charge between the points Q and $\mathrm{P} ; \mathrm{A}$ and B .
(c) Give the sign of the work done by the field in moving a small positive charge from Q to P .
(d) Give the sign of the work done by the external agency in moving a small negative charge from B to A.
(e) Does the kinetic energy of a small negative charge increase or decrease in going from B to A?
:::

:::figure{src="images/fig_physics-12-2_7.jpg" id="fig_physics-12-2_7"}
FIGURE 2.8
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
Give the signs of the potential difference $V_{\mathrm{P}}-V_{\mathrm{O}}$; $V_{\mathrm{B}}-V_{\mathrm{A}}$.
:::

:::solution
As $V \propto \frac{1}{r}, V_{P}>V_{Q}$. Thus, $\left(V_{P}-V_{Q}\right)$ is positive. Also $V_{B}$ is less negative than $V_{A}$. Thus, $V_{B}>V_{A}$ or ( $V_{B}-V_{A}$ ) is positive.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** both $\left(V_{P}-V_{O}\right)$ and $\left(V_{B}-V_{A}\right)$ are positive
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
Give the sign of the potential energy difference of a small negative charge between the points Q and $\mathrm{P} ; \mathrm{A}$ and B .
:::

:::solution
A small negative charge will be attracted towards positive charge. The negative charge moves from higher potential energy to lower potential energy. Therefore the sign of potential energy difference of a small negative charge between Q and P is positive.
Similarly, $(\text { P.E. })_{\mathrm{A}}>(\text { P.E. })_{\mathrm{B}}$ and hence sign of potential energy differences is positive.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** positive in both cases
:::

:::

:::part{label="c"}
:::prompt
Give the sign of the work done by the field in moving a small positive charge from Q to P .
:::

:::solution
In moving a small positive charge from Q to P , work has to be done by an external agency against the electric field. Therefore, work done by the field is negative.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** negative
:::

:::

:::part{label="d"}
:::prompt
Give the sign of the work done by the external agency in moving a small negative charge from B to A.
:::

:::solution
In moving a small negative charge from B to A work has to be done by the external agency. It is positive.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** positive
:::

:::

:::part{label="e"}
:::prompt
Does the kinetic energy of a small negative charge increase or decrease in going from B to A?
:::

:::solution
Due to force of repulsion on the negative charge, velocity decreases and hence the kinetic energy decreases in going from B to A.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** decreases

:::example{number="2.4" kind="example" id="ex_2.4" topic="Work to assemble charges on a square"}
#### Example 2.4

:::prompt
Four charges are arranged at the corners of a square ABCD of side $d$, as shown in Fig. 2.15. (a) Find the work required to put together this arrangement. (b) A charge $q_{0}$ is brought to the centre E of the square, the four charges being held fixed at its corners. How much extra work is needed to do this?
:::

:::figure{src="images/fig_physics-12-2_14.jpg" id="fig_physics-12-2_14"}
FIGURE 2.15
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
Find the work required to put together this arrangement.
:::

:::solution
The work done depends only on the final arrangement of the charges, not on how we bring them together. So we find the work needed for one way of assembling them: bring $+q$ to A first, then the charges $-q,+q$ and $-q$ to B, C and D respectively. We add up the work in four steps:
:::step{n="1"}
Bringing charge $+q$ to A, with no charge anywhere else, needs zero work.
:::

:::step{n="2"}
Bringing $-q$ to B, with $+q$ already at A, needs (charge at B) × (potential at B due to charge $+q$ at A):
$$
=-q \times \frac{q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d}=-\frac{q^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d}
$$
:::

:::step{n="3"}
Bringing charge $+q$ to C, with $+q$ at A and $-q$ at B, needs (charge at C) × (potential at C due to the charges at A and B):
$$
\begin{aligned}
& =+q \frac{+q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d \sqrt{2}}+\frac{-q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d} \\
& =\frac{-q^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d} 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::step{n="4"}
Bringing $-q$ to D, with $+q$ at $\mathrm{A},-q$ at B, and $+q$ at C, needs (charge at D$) \times($ tential at D due to the charges at A, B and C):
$$
\begin{aligned}
& =-q \frac{+q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d}+\frac{-q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d \sqrt{2}}+\frac{q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d} \\
& =\frac{-q^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d} 2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

Adding the work from steps (i)-(iv) gives the total work required:
$$
\begin{aligned}
& =\frac{-q^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d}(0)+(1)+1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\
& =\frac{-q^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d}(4-\sqrt{2})
\end{aligned}
$$
This depends only on how the charges are arranged, not on the order in which we bring them together — by definition, this is the total electrostatic energy of the charges.
(Students may try calculating the same energy in a different order and check that it comes out the same.)
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $\dfrac{-q^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} d}(4-\sqrt{2})$
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
A charge $q_{0}$ is brought to the centre E of the square, the four charges being held fixed at its corners. How much extra work is needed to do this?
:::

:::solution
The extra work necessary to bring a charge $q_{0}$ to the point E when the four charges are at $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{C}$ and D is $q_{0} \times$ (electrostatic potential at E due to the charges at $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{C}$ and D ). The electrostatic potential at E is clearly zero since potential due to A and C is cancelled by that due to B and D. Hence, no work is required to bring any charge to point E.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** zero
:::

:::example{number="2.5" kind="example" id="ex_2.5" topic="Potential energy of two charges in a field"}
#### Example 2.5

:::prompt
(a) Determine the electrostatic potential energy of a system consisting of two charges $7 \mu \mathrm{C}$ and $-2 \mu \mathrm{C}$ (and with no external field) placed at ( $-9 \mathrm{~cm}, 0,0$ ) and ( $9 \mathrm{~cm}, 0,0$ ) respectively.
(b) How much work is required to separate the two charges infinitely away from each other?
(c) Suppose that the same system of charges is now placed in an external electric field $E=A\left(1 / r^{2}\right) ; A=9 \times 10^{5} \mathrm{NC}^{-1} \mathrm{~m}^{2}$. What would the electrostatic energy of the configuration be?
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
Determine the electrostatic potential energy of a system consisting of two charges $7 \mu \mathrm{C}$ and $-2 \mu \mathrm{C}$ (and with no external field) placed at ( $-9 \mathrm{~cm}, 0,0$ ) and ( $9 \mathrm{~cm}, 0,0$ ) respectively.
:::

:::solution
$U=\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{q_{1} q_{2}}{r}=9 \times 10^{9} \times \frac{7 \times(-2) \times 10^{-12}}{0.18}=-0.7 \mathrm{~J}$.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $U=-0.7 \mathrm{~J}$
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
How much work is required to separate the two charges infinitely away from each other?
:::

:::solution
$W=U_{2}-U_{1}=0-U=0-(-0.7)=0.7 \mathrm{~J}$.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $W=0.7 \mathrm{~J}$
:::

:::

:::part{label="c"}
:::prompt
Suppose that the same system of charges is now placed in an external electric field $E=A\left(1 / r^{2}\right) ; A=9 \times 10^{5} \mathrm{NC}^{-1} \mathrm{~m}^{2}$. What would the electrostatic energy of the configuration be?
:::

:::solution
The mutual interaction energy of the two charges remains unchanged. In addition, there is the energy of interaction of the two charges with the external electric field. We find,
$$
q_{1} V\left(\mathbf{r}_{1}\right)+q_{2} V\left(\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)=A \frac{7 \mu \mathrm{C}}{0.09 \mathrm{~m}}+A \frac{-2 \mu \mathrm{C}}{0.09 \mathrm{~m}}
$$
and the net electrostatic energy is
$$
\begin{array}{r}
q_{1} V\left(\mathbf{r}_{1}\right)+q_{2} V\left(\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)+\frac{q_{1} q_{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r_{12}}=A \frac{7 \mu \mathrm{C}}{0.09 \mathrm{~m}}+A \frac{-2 \mu \mathrm{C}}{0.09 \mathrm{~m}}-0.7 \mathrm{~J} \\
=70-20-0.7=49.3 \mathrm{~J}
\end{array}
$$
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $49.3 \mathrm{~J}$
:::

:::example{number="2.6" kind="example" id="ex_2.6" topic="Heat released reorienting dipoles in a field"}
#### Example 2.6

:::prompt
A molecule of a substance has a permanent electric dipole moment of magnitude $10^{-29} \mathrm{C} \mathrm{m}$. A mole of this substance is polarised (at low temperature) by applying a strong electrostatic field of magnitude $10^{6} \mathrm{~V} \mathrm{~m}^{-1}$. The direction of the field is suddenly changed by an angle of 60°. Estimate the heat released by the substance in aligning its dipoles along the new direction of the field. For simplicity, assume 100\% polarisation of the sample.
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
:::concept{label="Given"}
The dipole moment of each molecule is $=10^{-29} \mathrm{C} \mathrm{m}$. One mole of the substance has $6 \times 10^{23}$ molecules, so the total dipole moment of all the molecules is $p=6 \times 10^{23} \times 10^{-29} \mathrm{C} \mathrm{m}$ $=6 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{C} \mathrm{m}$.
:::

:::step{n="1" label="Substitute"}
Initial potential energy, $U_{i}=-p E \cos \theta=-6 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^{6} \cos 0^{\circ}=-6 \mathrm{~J}$. Final potential energy (when $\theta=60^{\circ}$), $U_{f}=-6 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^{6} \cos 60^{\circ}=-3 \mathrm{~J}$. Change in potential energy $=-3 \mathrm{~J}-(-6 \mathrm{~J})=3 \mathrm{~J}$.
:::

:::step{n="2" label="Conclusion"}
So there is a loss in potential energy — this must be the energy released by the substance as heat while its dipoles align.
:::
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $3 \mathrm{~J}$
:::

:::example{number="2.7" kind="example" id="ex_2.7" topic="Conductors, insulators and electrostatic safety"}
#### Example 2.7

:::prompt
(a) A comb run through one's dry hair attracts small bits of paper. Why?
What happens if the hair is wet or if it is a rainy day? (Remember, a paper does not conduct electricity.)
(b) Ordinary rubber is an insulator. But special rubber tyres of aircraft are made slightly conducting. Why is this necessary?
(c) Vehicles carrying inflammable materials usually have metallic ropes touching the ground during motion. Why?
(d) A bird perches on a bare high power line, and nothing happens to the bird. A man standing on the ground touches the same line and gets a fatal shock. Why?
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
A comb run through one's dry hair attracts small bits of paper. Why? What happens if the hair is wet or if it is a rainy day? (Remember, a paper does not conduct electricity.)
:::

:::solution
This is because the comb gets charged by friction. The molecules in the paper gets polarised by the charged comb, resulting in a net force of attraction. If the hair is wet, or if it is rainy day, friction between hair and the comb reduces. The comb does not get charged and thus it will not attract small bits of paper.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** charging by friction polarises the paper's molecules, giving a net attractive force; wet hair reduces friction, so the comb no longer charges and no longer attracts the paper
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
Ordinary rubber is an insulator. But special rubber tyres of aircraft are made slightly conducting. Why is this necessary?
:::

:::solution
To let the charge built up by friction flow safely to the ground. If too much static electricity builds up, it can spark and start a fire.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** to conduct away friction-produced charge before it sparks and causes fire
:::

:::

:::part{label="c"}
:::prompt
Vehicles carrying inflammable materials usually have metallic ropes touching the ground during motion. Why?
:::

:::solution
Reason similar to (b).
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** same reason as (b) - to conduct away friction-produced charge safely to the ground
:::

:::

:::part{label="d"}
:::prompt
A bird perches on a bare high power line, and nothing happens to the bird. A man standing on the ground touches the same line and gets a fatal shock. Why?
:::

:::solution
Current passes only when there is difference in potential.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** the bird's two feet are at (nearly) the same potential, so no current flows through it; the man, in contact with the line and the ground, spans a large potential difference
:::

:::example{number="2.8" kind="example" id="ex_2.8" topic="Capacitance with a partial dielectric slab"}
#### Example 2.8

:::prompt
A slab of material of dielectric constant $K$ has the same area as the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor but has a thickness $(3 / 4) d$, where $d$ is the separation of the plates. How is the capacitance changed when the slab is inserted between the plates?
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
Let $E_{0}=V_{0} / d$ be the electric field between the plates when there is no dielectric and the potential difference is $V_{0}$. If the dielectric is now inserted, the electric field in the dielectric will be $E=E_{0} / K$. The potential difference will then be
$$
\begin{aligned}
& V=E_{0}\left(\frac{1}{4} d\right)+\frac{E_{0}}{K}\left(\frac{3}{4} d\right) \\
& =E_{0} d\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{3}{4 K}\right)=V_{0} \frac{K+3}{4 K}
\end{aligned}
$$
The potential difference decreases by the factor $(K+3) / 4 K$ while the free charge $Q_{0}$ on the plates remains unchanged. The capacitance thus increases

:::formula{label="Capacitance with the slab"}
$$
C=\frac{Q_{0}}{V}=\frac{4 K}{K+3} \frac{Q_{0}}{V_{0}}=\frac{4 K}{K+3} C_{0}
$$
:::
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $C=\dfrac{4K}{K+3}C_0$
:::

:::

:::example{number="2.9" kind="example" id="ex_2.9" topic="Equivalent capacitance and charge in a network"}
#### Example 2.9

:::prompt
A network of four $10 \mu \mathrm{~F}$ capacitors is connected to a 500 V supply, as shown in Fig. 2.29. Determine (a) the equivalent capacitance of the network and (b) the charge on each capacitor. (Note, the charge on a capacitor is the charge on the plate with higher potential, equal and opposite to the charge on the plate with lower potential.)
:::

:::figure{src="images/fig_physics-12-2_28.jpg" id="fig_physics-12-2_28"}
FIGURE 2.29
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
Determine the equivalent capacitance of the network.
:::

:::solution
In the given network, $C_{1}, C_{2}$ and $C_{3}$ are connected in series. The effective capacitance $\mathrm{C}^{\prime}$ of these three capacitors is given by

:::formula{label="Key formula"}
$$
\frac{1}{C^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{C_{1}}+\frac{1}{C_{2}}+\frac{1}{C_{3}}
$$
:::
For $C_{1}=C_{2}=C_{3}=10 \mu \mathrm{~F}, C^{\prime}=(10 / 3) \mu \mathrm{~F}$. The network has $C^{\prime}$ and $C_{4}$ connected in parallel. Thus, the equivalent capacitance $C$ of the network is
$$
C=C^{\prime}+C_{4}=\frac{10}{3}+10 \quad \mu \mathrm{~F}=13.3 \mu \mathrm{~F}
$$
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $C=13.3 \mu \mathrm{~F}$
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
Determine the charge on each capacitor.
:::

:::solution
:::step{n="1"}
Clearly, from the figure, the charge on each of the capacitors, $C_{1}$, $C_{2}$ and $C_{3}$ is the same, say $Q$. Let the charge on $C_{4}$ be $Q^{\prime}$. Now, since the potential difference across AB is $Q / C_{1}$, across BC is $Q / C_{2}$, across CD is $Q / C_{3}$, we have
$$
\frac{Q}{C_{1}}+\frac{Q}{C_{2}}+\frac{Q}{C_{3}}=500 \mathrm{~V}
$$
Also, $Q^{\prime} / C_{4}=500 \mathrm{~V}$.
:::

:::step{n="2"}
This gives for the given value of the capacitances,
$$
\begin{aligned}
& Q=500 \mathrm{~V} \times \frac{10}{3} \mu \mathrm{~F}=1.7 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{C} \text { and } \\
& Q^{\prime}=500 \mathrm{~V} \times 10 \mu \mathrm{~F}=5.0 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{C}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $Q=1.7 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{C}$ on $C_1$, $C_2$, $C_3$; $Q'=5.0 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{C}$ on $C_4$
:::

:::example{number="2.10" kind="example" id="ex_2.10" topic="Energy stored when capacitors are connected"}
#### Example 2.10

:::prompt
(a) A 900 pF capacitor is charged by 100 V battery [Fig. 2.31(a)]. How much electrostatic energy is stored by the capacitor? (b) The capacitor is disconnected from the battery and connected to another 900 pF capacitor [Fig. 2.31(b)]. What is the electrostatic energy stored by the system?
:::

:::figure{src="images/fig_physics-12-2_30.jpg" id="fig_physics-12-2_30"}
FIGURE 2.31
:::

:::part{label="a"}
:::prompt
A 900 pF capacitor is charged by 100 V battery [Fig. 2.31(a)]. How much electrostatic energy is stored by the capacitor?
:::

:::solution
The charge on the capacitor is
$$
Q=C V=900 \times 10^{-12} \mathrm{~F} \times 100 \mathrm{~V}=9 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C}
$$
The energy stored by the capacitor is
$$
\begin{aligned}
& =(1 / 2) \mathrm{CV}^{2}=(1 / 2) Q V \\
& =(1 / 2) \times 9 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C} \times 100 \mathrm{~V}=4.5 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $4.5 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}$
:::

:::

:::part{label="b"}
:::prompt
The capacitor is disconnected from the battery and connected to another 900 pF capacitor [Fig. 2.31(b)]. What is the electrostatic energy stored by the system?
:::

:::solution
In the steady state, both capacitors have their positive plates at the same potential, and their negative plates at the same potential. Let this common potential difference be $\mathrm{V}^{\prime}$, so the charge on each capacitor is $Q^{\prime}=C V^{\prime}$. Charge conservation gives $Q^{\prime}=Q / 2$, so $V^{\prime}=V / 2$. The total energy of the system is
$$
=2 \times \frac{1}{2} Q^{\prime} V^{\prime}=\frac{1}{4} Q V=2.25 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}
$$
So no charge is lost in going from (a) to (b), but the final energy is only half the initial energy.

:::note{type="tip" label="Where did the energy go?"}
There is a brief transient period before the system settles into (b). During this time, a transient current flows from the first capacitor to the second, and energy is lost as heat and electromagnetic radiation.
:::
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $2.25 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}$ (half the initial energy; the rest is lost as heat and electromagnetic radiation during the transient)
:::

## Questions and Solutions

:::question{number="2.1" kind="exercise" id="q_2.1" topic="Zero-potential point between two charges"}
#### Question 2.1

:::prompt
Two charges $5 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C}$ and $-3 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C}$ are located 16 cm apart. At what point(s) on the line joining the two charges is the electric potential zero? Take the potential at infinity to be zero.
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
:::concept{label="Given"}
There are two charges,
$$
\begin{aligned}
& q_{1}=5 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C} \\
& q_{2}=-3 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{C}
\end{aligned}
$$
Distance between the two charges, $d=16 \mathrm{~cm}=0.16 \mathrm{~m}$
Let the electric potential ( $V$ ) at point P be zero. Potential at point P is the sum of potentials caused by charges $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ respectively.
$$
\therefore V=\frac{q_{1}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0} r}+\frac{q_{2}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0}(d-r)}
$$
Where,
$$
\epsilon_{0}=\text { Permittivity of free space }
$$
:::

:::step{n="1"}
Consider a point P on the line joining the two charges, as shown in the given figure. $r=$ Distance of point P from charge $q_{1}$.

:::figure{src="images/fig_physics-12-2_31.jpg" id="fig_physics-12-2_31"}
:::

For $V=0$, equation (i) reduces to
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{q_{1}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0} r}=-\frac{q_{2}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0}(d-r)} \\
& \frac{q_{1}}{r}=\frac{-q_{2}}{d-r} \\
& \frac{5 \times 10^{-8}}{r}=-\frac{\left(-3 \times 10^{-8}\right)}{(0.16-r)} \\
& \frac{0.16}{r}-1=\frac{3}{5} \\
& \frac{0.16}{r}=\frac{8}{5} \\
& \therefore r=0.1 \mathrm{~m}=10 \mathrm{~cm}
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, the potential is zero at a distance of 10 cm from the positive charge between the charges.

$$
\begin{aligned}

& \frac{0.16}{s}=\frac{2}{5} \\
& \therefore s=0.4 \mathrm{~m}=40 \mathrm{~cm}
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, the potential is zero at a distance of 40 cm from the positive charge outside the system of charges.

:::answer
**Answer:** zero at 10 cm from the positive charge (between the charges) and at 40 cm from the positive charge (outside the system, beyond the negative charge)

#### Question 2.5

:::prompt
A parallel plate capacitor with air between the plates has a capacitance of 8 pF ( $1 \mathrm{pF}=10^{-12} \mathrm{~F}$ ). What will be the capacitance if the distance between the plates is reduced by half, and the space between them is filled with a substance of dielectric constant 6?
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
Capacitance between the parallel plates of the capacitor, $\mathrm{C}=8 \mathrm{pF}$
Initially, distance between the parallel plates was $d$ and it was filled with air. Dielectric constant of air, $k=1$
Capacitance, $C$, is given by the formula,

:::formula{label="Capacitance with air"}

\end{aligned}

Dielectric constant of the substance filled in between the plates, $k^{\prime}=6$

:::formula{label="Capacitance with dielectric, halved gap"}

C^{\prime}=\frac{k^{\prime} \in_{0} A}{d^{\prime}}=\frac{6 \in_{0} A}{\frac{d}{2}}
$$
:::
Taking ratios of equations (i) and (ii), we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
C^{\prime} & =2 \times 6 C \\
& =12 C \\
& =12 \times 8=96 \mathrm{pF}
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, the capacitance between the plates is 96 pF.

:::answer
**Answer:** $96 \mathrm{pF}$

:::question{number="2.11" kind="exercise" id="q_2.11" topic="Energy lost connecting two capacitors"}
#### Question 2.11

:::prompt
A 600pF capacitor is charged by a 200V supply. It is then disconnected from the supply and is connected to another uncharged 600 pF capacitor. How much electrostatic energy is lost in the process?
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
Capacitance of the capacitor, $C=600 \mathrm{pF}$
Potential difference, $V=200 \mathrm{~V}$
Electrostatic energy stored in the capacitor is given by,

:::formula{label="Key formula"}
$$
\begin{aligned}
E & =\frac{1}{2} C V^{2} \\
& =\frac{1}{2} \times\left(600 \times 10^{-12}\right) \times(200)^{2} \\
& =1.2 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~J}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::
If supply is disconnected from the capacitor and another capacitor of capacitance $C=600$ pF is connected to it, then equivalent capacitance $\left(C^{\prime}\right)$ of the combination is given by,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{C^{\prime}} & =\frac{1}{C}+\frac{1}{C} \\
& =\frac{1}{600}+\frac{1}{600}=\frac{2}{600}=\frac{1}{300} \\
\therefore C^{\prime} & =300 \mathrm{pF}
\end{aligned}
$$
New electrostatic energy can be calculated as
$$
\begin{aligned}
E^{\prime} & =\frac{1}{2} \times C^{\prime} \times V^{2} \\
& =\frac{1}{2} \times 300 \times(200)^{2} \\
& =0.6 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~J}
\end{aligned}
$$
Loss in electrostatic energy $=E-E^{\prime}$
$$
\begin{aligned}
& =1.2 \times 10^{-5}-0.6 \times 10^{-5} \\
& =0.6 \times 10^{-5} \\
& =6 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, the electrostatic energy lost in the process is $6 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}$.
:::

:::answer
**Answer:** $6 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~J}$
