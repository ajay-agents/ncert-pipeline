Tetrahedral complexes do not show geometrical isomerism. This is because the unidentate ligands attached to the central metal atom always have the same relative positions with respect to each other.

:::example{number="5.7" kind="example" id="ex_5.7" topic="Predicting geometry from magnetic moment" simplified="True"}
#### Example 5.7

:::prompt
The spin only magnetic moment of $\left[\mathrm{MnBr}_{4}\right]^{2-}$ is 5.9 BM. Predict the geometry of the complex ion ?
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
:::step{n="1" label="Given"}
The coordination number of $\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}$ ion in the complex ion is 4. So the complex ion will be either tetrahedral ( $s p^{3}$ hybridisation) or square planar ( $d s p^{2}$ hybridisation).
:::

:::step{n="2" label="Substitute"}
The magnetic moment of the complex ion is 5.9 BM. This value matches five unpaired electrons in the $d$ orbitals. A tetrahedral shape allows five unpaired electrons, but a square planar shape does not.
:::

:::step{n="3" label="Conclusion"}
So the complex ion is tetrahedral in shape, not square planar.
:::

## Questions and Solutions

:::question{number="5.1" kind="exercise" id="q_5.1" topic="Werner's postulates and bonding" simplified="True"}
#### Question 5.1

:::prompt
Explain the bonding in coordination compounds in terms of Werner's postulates.
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
Werner's postulates explain the bonding in coordination compounds as follows:

(i) A metal shows two types of valencies: primary and secondary. Negative ions satisfy primary valencies. Both negative ions and neutral molecules can satisfy secondary valencies.

:::note{type="tip" label="Modern terminology"}
(In modern terms, primary valency is the oxidation number of the metal ion. Secondary valency is the coordination number of the metal ion.
:::

(ii) A metal ion has a fixed number of secondary valencies around the central atom. These valencies point in specific directions in space, giving the coordination compound its definite geometry.
(iii) Primary valencies are usually ionizable. Secondary valencies are non-ionizable.
:::

:::question{number="5.2" kind="exercise" id="q_5.2" topic="Complex formation vs simple ion tests" simplified="True"}
#### Question 5.2

:::prompt
$\mathrm{FeSO}_{4}$ solution mixed with $\left(\mathrm{NH}_{4}\right)_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ solution in 1:1 molar ratio gives the test of $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ ion but $\mathrm{CuSO}_{4}$ solution mixed with aqueous ammonia in 1:4 molar ratio does not give the test of $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ ion. Explain why?
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
:::formula
$$
\left(\mathrm{NH}_{4}\right)_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{FeSO}_{4}+6 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{FeSO}_{4} \cdot\left(\mathrm{NH}_{4}\right)_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 6 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}
$$
:::

Mohr's salt

:::formula
$$
\mathrm{CuSO}_{4}+4 \mathrm{NH}_{3}+5 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O} \longrightarrow\left[\mathrm{Cu}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}\right] \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 5 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}
$$
:::

tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate
Both $\quad \mathrm{FeSO}_{4} \cdot\left(\mathrm{NH}_{4}\right)_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 6 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ and $\left[\mathrm{Cu}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}\right] \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 5 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ are addition compounds. The only major difference is that the former is a double salt, while the latter is a coordination compound.

A double salt is an addition compound. It is stable in the solid state, but breaks up into its constituent ions when dissolved. These compounds show the individual properties of their constituents. For example, $\mathrm{FeSO}_{4} \cdot\left(\mathrm{NH}_{4}\right)_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 6 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ breaks into $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}, \mathrm{NH}^{4+}$ and $\mathrm{SO}_{4}{ }^{2-}$ ions. So it gives a positive test for $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ ions. A coordination compound is an addition compound that keeps its identity both in the solid state and in solution. Here, the individual properties of the constituents are lost. Since $\left[\mathrm{Cu}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}\right] \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 5 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ retains its identity (as the complex ion) in solution, it does not show the test for $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$. The ions present in the $\left[\mathrm{Cu}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}\right] \mathrm{SO}_{4} \cdot 5 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ are $\left[\mathrm{Cu}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}\right]^{2+}$ and $\mathrm{SO}_{4}{ }^{2-}$.
:::

:::question{number="5.3" kind="exercise" id="q_5.3" topic="Basic coordination compound terminology" simplified="True"}
#### Question 5.3

:::prompt
Explain with two examples each of the following: coordination entity, ligand, coordination number, coordination polyhedron, homoleptic and heteroleptic.
:::

:::part{label="(i)"}
:::solution
:::concept{label="Coordination entity"}
Coordination entity:

A coordination entity constitutes a central metal atom or ion bonded to a fixed number of ions or molecules; it may be cationic, anionic, or neutral overall. In a coordination entity, a suitable number of neutral molecules or negative ions (called ligands) surround the central atom or ion. For example:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
{\left[\mathrm{Ni}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{2+},\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}} & =\text { cationic complex } \\
{\left[\mathrm{PtCl}_{4}\right]^{2-},\left[\mathrm{Ag}(\mathrm{CN})_{2}\right]^{-}} & =\text {anionic complex } \\
{\left[\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_{4}\right],\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4} \mathrm{Cl}_{2}\right]} & =\text { neutral complex }
\end{array}
$$
:::
:::

:::

:::part{label="(ii)"}
:::solution
:::concept{label="Ligands"}
Ligands

Neutral molecules or negatively charged ions that surround the metal atom in a coordination entity or a coordination complex are called ligands. For example,
$\ddot{\mathrm{N}} \mathrm{H}_{3}, \mathrm{H}_{2} \ddot{\mathrm{O}}, \mathrm{Cl}^{-},-\mathrm{OH}$. Ligands are usually polar and have at least one unshared pair of valence electrons.
:::
:::

:::

:::part{label="(iii)"}
:::solution
:::concept{label="Coordination number"}
Coordination number:

The coordination number of a metal atom is the total number of ligands (neutral molecules or negative ions) attached to it in the coordination sphere. It is also called its ligancy.

For example:

(a) In the complex $\mathrm{K}_{2}\left[\mathrm{PtCl}_{6}\right]$, six chloride ions are attached to Pt in the coordination sphere. So the coordination number of Pt is 6.
(b) Similarly, in the complex $\left[\mathrm{Ni}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}\right] \mathrm{Cl}_{2}$, the coordination number of the central atom $(\mathrm{Ni})$ is 4.
:::
:::

:::

:::part{label="(iv)"}
:::solution
:::concept{label="Coordination polyhedron"}
Coordination polyhedron:

The coordination polyhedron is the spatial arrangement of the ligands that are directly attached to the central metal ion in the coordination sphere. For example:

(a) $\left[\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_{4}\right]$ is tetrahedral.
(b) $\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$ is octahedral.
:::
:::

:::

:::part{label="(v)"}
:::solution
:::concept{label="Homoleptic complexes"}
Homoleptic complexes:

These are complexes in which the metal ion is bound to only one kind of donor group. For example: $\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+},\left[\mathrm{PtCl}_{4}\right]^{2-}$ etc.
:::
:::

:::

:::part{label="(vi)"}
:::solution
:::concept{label="Heteroleptic complexes"}
Heteroleptic complexes:

These are complexes in which the central metal ion is bound to more than one type of donor group.

For example: $\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4} \mathrm{Cl}_{2}\right]^{+},\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{5} \mathrm{Cl}\right]^{2+}$
:::
:::

Ambidentate ligands:

Ligands that can attach themselves to the central metal atom through two different atoms are called ambidentate ligands. For example:

(a) Nitrite ion, $\mathrm{NO}_{2}^{-}$: can coordinate either through the nitrogen atom (nitro, $-\mathrm{NO}_{2}$) or through an oxygen atom (nitrito, $-\mathrm{ONO}$).

(b) Thiocyanate ion, $\mathrm{SCN}^{-}$: can coordinate through the sulphur atom (thiocyanato, $-\mathrm{SCN}$) or through the nitrogen atom (isothiocyanato, $-\mathrm{NCS}$).

\end{array}

:::question{number="5.18" kind="exercise" id="q_5.18" topic="Crystal field splitting energy" simplified="True"}
#### Question 5.18

:::prompt
What is crystal field splitting energy? How does the magnitude of $\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}$ decide the actual configuration of $d$ orbitals in a coordination entity?
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
:::concept{label="Crystal field splitting energy"}
The degenerate $d$-orbitals (in a spherical field environment) split into two levels, $e_{g}$ and $t_{2 \mathrm{~g}}$, when ligands are present. This splitting is called crystal-field splitting. The energy difference between the two levels ( $e_{\mathrm{g}}$ and $t_{2 \mathrm{~g}}$ ) is called the crystal-field splitting energy. It is denoted by $\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}$.
:::

After the orbitals split, electrons start filling them. First, 1 electron each fills the three $t_{2 \mathrm{~g}}$ orbitals. Then the fourth electron can go in two ways: it can enter the $e_{\mathrm{g}}$ orbital, giving an $t_{2 \mathrm{~g}}{ }^{3} e_{\mathrm{g}}{ }^{1}$-like electronic configuration, or it can pair up in the $t_{2 g}$ orbitals, giving an $t_{2 g}{ }^{4} e_{g}{ }^{0}$-like electronic configuration. If the $\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}$ value of a ligand is less than the pairing energy ( P ), the electron enters the $e_{\mathrm{g}}$ orbital. If the $\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}$ value of a ligand is more than the pairing energy (P), the electron enters the $t_{2 \mathrm{~g}}$ orbital.
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
The colour of a coordination compound depends on the magnitude of the crystal-field splitting energy, $\Delta$. This CFSE in turn depends on the nature of the ligand. In the case of $\left[\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN})_{6}\right]^{4-}$ and $\left[\mathrm{Fe}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)_{6}\right]^{2+}$, the colour differs because the CFSE differs. Now, $\mathrm{CN}^{-}$ is a strong field ligand with a higher CFSE value than water. This means the energy absorbed for the intra $d-d$ transition also differs. Hence, the transmitted colour also differs.

:::question{number="5.22" kind="exercise" id="q_5.22" topic="Bonding in metal carbonyls" simplified="True" corrections_applied="2"}

Discuss the nature of bonding in metal carbonyls.
:::

:::figure{src="images/fig_5_194.jpg" id="fig_5_194"}
Synergic bonding in metal carbonyls
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
The metal-carbon bonds in metal carbonyls have both $\sigma$ and $\pi$ character. A $\sigma$ bond forms when the carbonyl carbon donates a lone pair of electrons to a vacant orbital on the metal. A $\pi$ bond forms when a pair of electrons from the filled metal $d$ orbital is donated into the vacant anti-bonding $\pi^{*}$ orbital (this is also called back bonding of the carbonyl group). The $\sigma$ bond strengthens the $\pi$ bond, and vice versa. This creates a synergic effect from the metal-ligand bonding, which strengthens the bond between CO and the metal.
:::

:::question{number="5.25" kind="exercise" id="q_5.25" topic="Violet colour of titanium complex (CFT)"}
#### Question 5.25

:::prompt
Explain the violet colour of the complex $\left[\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$ on the basis of crystal field theory.
:::

:::solution{label="Solution"}
:::note{type="caution" label="Not in official solutions manual"}
This answer was computed for completeness, since the official English solutions manual does not contain a solution for this question.
:::

:::concept{label="Given"}
In $\left[\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$, $\mathrm{Ti}^{3+}$ has a single $3d$ electron ($3d^{1}$ configuration). Under the octahedral crystal field of the six water ligands, the five degenerate $3d$ orbitals split into the lower-energy $t_{2g}$ set and the higher-energy $e_g$ set; in the ground state, this single electron occupies one of the $t_{2g}$ orbitals.
:::

:::concept{label="d-d transition"}
When white light falls on the complex, $\left[\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$ absorbs light of the energy needed to promote this electron from the $t_{2g}$ level to the $e_g$ level (a $d$-$d$ transition); this absorbed energy corresponds to light in the yellow-green region of the visible spectrum (crystal-field splitting energy $\Delta_{o}$ for this ion, around 20,300 cm$^{-1}$).
:::

:::step{label="Conclusion"}
Since the yellow-green component of white light is absorbed, the complementary colour, violet, is transmitted - which is the colour observed for aqueous solutions of $\left[\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$.

:::table
| $\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$ | $\left[\mathrm{Ni}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{2+}$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| Oxidation state of cobalt $=+3$ | Oxidation state of $\mathrm{Ni}=+2$ |
| Electronic configuration of cobalt $=d^{6}$ | Electronic configuration of nickel $=d^{8}$ |
:::





For $\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$: $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ is a strong field ligand, so it pairs up the six $3d$ electrons of $\mathrm{Co}^{3+}$ ($d^6$), giving $t_{2g}{}^{6}e_g{}^{0}$. This leaves two $3d$ orbitals vacant, so $\mathrm{Co}^{3+}$ undergoes $d^{2}sp^{3}$ hybridisation using the two now-empty inner $3d$ orbitals. Hence $\left[\mathrm{Co}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{3+}$ is an inner orbital complex (diamagnetic).

For $\left[\mathrm{Ni}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{2+}$: $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ has a $d^8$ configuration, so even with a strong field ligand the eight electrons must occupy all five $3d$ orbitals ($t_{2g}{}^{6}e_g{}^{2}$, 2 unpaired) - no $3d$ orbital is left vacant. $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ therefore cannot undergo $d^{2}sp^{3}$ hybridisation and instead uses the outer $4d$ orbitals, undergoing $sp^{3}d^{2}$ hybridisation. Hence $\left[\mathrm{Ni}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{6}\right]^{2+}$ forms an outer orbital complex (paramagnetic, 2 unpaired electrons).
