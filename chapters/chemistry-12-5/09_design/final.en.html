Werner's postulates and bonding

Explain the bonding in coordination compounds in terms of Werner's postulates.

Given
Werner's postulates explain the bonding in coordination compounds as follows:
(i) A metal shows two types of valencies: primary and secondary.
Negative ions satisfy primary valencies.
Both negative ions and neutral molecules can satisfy secondary valencies.
Modern terminology
(In modern terms, primary valency is the oxidation number of the metal ion.
Secondary valency is the coordination number of the metal ion.
Conclusion
(ii) A metal ion has a fixed number of secondary valencies around the central atom.
These valencies point in specific directions in space, giving the coordination compound its definite geometry.
(iii) Primary valencies are usually ionizable.
Secondary valencies are non-ionizable.

The coordination number of a metal atom is the total number of ligands (neutral molecules or negative ions) attached to it in the coordination sphere.

Crystal field splitting energy

What is crystal field splitting energy? How does the magnitude of Δo decide the actual configuration of d orbitals in a coordination entity?

Given
The degenerate d-orbitals (in a spherical field environment) split into two levels, eg and t2 g, when ligands are present. This splitting is called crystal-field splitting. The energy difference between the two levels ( eg and t2 g ) is called the crystal-field splitting energy. It is denoted by Δo.
Conclusion
After the orbitals split, electrons start filling them. First, 1 electron each fills the three t2 g orbitals. Then the fourth electron can go in two ways: it can enter the eg orbital, giving an t2 g 3 eg 1-like electronic configuration, or it can pair up in the t2 g orbitals, giving an t2 g 4 eg 0-like electronic configuration. If the Δo value of a ligand is less than the pairing energy ( P ), the electron enters the eg orbital. If the Δo value of a ligand is more than the pairing energy (P), the electron enters the t2 g orbital.

Bonding in metal carbonyls

Discuss the nature of bonding in metal carbonyls.

Conclusion
The metal-carbon bonds in metal carbonyls have both σ and π character. A σ bond forms when the carbonyl carbon donates a lone pair of electrons to a vacant orbital on the metal. A π bond forms when a pair of electrons from the filled metal d orbital is donated into the vacant anti-bonding π* orbital (this is also called back bonding of the carbonyl group). The σ bond strengthens the π bond, and vice versa. This creates a synergic effect from the metal-ligand bonding, which strengthens the bond between CO and the metal.
