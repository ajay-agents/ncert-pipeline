4.1
Transition element vs non-transition element

On what ground can you say that scandium (Z = 21) is a transition element but zinc (Z = 30) is not?

Conclusion
Scandium has incompletely filled 3 d orbitals in its ground state (3 d1). So it is regarded as a transition element. Zinc, on the other hand, has completely filled d orbitals (3 d10) in its ground state as well as in its oxidised state. So it is not regarded as a transition element.

4.2
High enthalpy of atomisation

Why do the transition elements exhibit higher enthalpies of atomisation?

Conclusion
Transition elements have a large number of unpaired electrons in their atoms.
This gives stronger interatomic interaction and hence stronger bonding between atoms.
As a result, they have higher enthalpies of atomisation.

Transition element definition

Silver atom has completely filled d orbitals ( 4 d10 ) in its ground state. How can you say that it is a transition element?

Conclusion
Ag has a completely filled 4 d orbital ( 4 d10 5 s1 ) in its ground state. Silver shows two oxidation states, +1 and +2. In the +1 state, one electron is removed from the s-orbital. In the +2 state, an electron is removed from the d-orbital. This makes the d-orbital incomplete ( 4 d9 ). So, silver is a transition element.

2
Enthalpy of atomisation of zinc

In the series Sc(Z = 21) to Zn(Z = 30), the enthalpy of atomisation of zinc is the lowest, i.e., 126 kJ mol-1. Why?

Conclusion
The enthalpy of atomization depends on how strong the metallic bonding is. The stronger the metallic bonding, the higher the enthalpy of atomization. All transition metals, except Zn (electronic configuration: 3 d10 4 s2 ), have some unpaired electrons. These unpaired electrons give stronger metallic bonding. Zn has no such unpaired electrons, so its inter-atomic bonding is the weakest. This is why Zn has the lowest enthalpy of atomization.

5
Irregular ionisation enthalpies

How would you account for the irregular variation of ionisation enthalpies (first and second) in the first series of the transition elements?

Conclusion
Ionization enthalpies increase across the series because the inner d-orbitals keep filling up. Some configurations, such as d0, d5, d10, are extra stable. Elements with these configurations have very high ionization enthalpies.
For the first ionization energy, Cr has a low value. This is because after losing one electron, Cr reaches the stable configuration (3 d5). Zn, on the other hand, has an exceptionally high first ionization energy, because an electron has to be removed from its stable, fully-filled orbitals (3 d10 4 s2).
Second ionization energies are higher than first ionization energies, since it is harder to remove an electron once one electron is already gone. Cr and Cu also have exceptionally high second ionization energies, because after losing the first electron, they reach the stable configurations (Cr+: 3 d5. and .Cu+: 3 d10). Removing one more electron from this stable configuration needs a lot of energy.

10
Actinoid vs lanthanoid contraction

Actinoid contraction is greater from element to element than lanthanoid contraction.

Why?

Conclusion
In actinoids, 5 f orbitals are filled. These 5 f orbitals have a poorer shielding effect than 4 f orbitals (in lanthanoids). So, the effective nuclear charge on valence-shell electrons is much higher in actinoids than in lanthanoids. This is why the size contraction is greater in actinoids than in lanthanoids.

Mn2+ vs Fe2+ stability to oxidation

Why are Mn2+ compounds more stable than Fe2+ towards oxidation to their +3 state?

Conclusion
Electronic configuration of Mn2+ is [Ar]18 3 d5. Electronic configuration of Fe2+ is [Ar]18 3 d6.
Half-filled and fully-filled orbitals are more stable. So Mn in the (+2) state has a stable d5 configuration. This is why Mn2+ resists oxidation to Mn3+.
Fe2+ has 3 d6 configuration. By losing one electron, it changes to a more stable 3 d5 configuration. So Fe2+ easily gets oxidized to the Fe+3 oxidation state.

Electronic configuration and oxidation state stability

To what extent do the electronic configurations decide the stability of oxidation states in the first series of the transition elements? Illustrate your answer with examples.

Conclusion
The elements in the first half of the transition series show many oxidation states. Mn shows the maximum number, from +2 to +7. The stability of the +2 oxidation state increases as the atomic number increases, because more electrons are filling the d-orbital.
Sc, however, does not show a +2 oxidation state. Its electronic configuration is 4 s2 3 d1. It loses all three electrons to form Sc3+. The +3 state of Sc is very stable because, by losing all three electrons, it gets the stable noble gas configuration [Ar]. Ti (+4) and V(+5) are very stable for the same reason.
For Mn, the +2 state is very stable because after losing two electrons, its d-orbital is exactly half-filled, [Ar] 3 d5.

7
Lanthanoid contraction and its consequences

What is lanthanoid contraction? What are the consequences of lanthanoid contraction?

Conclusion
As we move along the lanthanoid series, the atomic number increases by one each time.
So the number of electrons and protons in the atom also increases by one.
Since the new electrons are added to the same shell, the effective nuclear charge increases.
This is because the increase in nuclear attraction from the added proton is greater than the increase in repulsion from the added electron.
Also, as the atomic number increases, the number of electrons in the 4 f orbital increases too. These 4 f electrons shield poorly. So the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons increases, and the nucleus pulls the outermost electrons in more strongly.
This causes a steady decrease in the size of the lanthanoids as the atomic number increases.
This is called lanthanoid contraction.
## Consequences of lanthanoid contraction
(i) It explains the similarity in the properties of the second and third transition series.
(ii) It makes it possible to separate the lanthanoids from each other.
(iii) It causes the basic strength of the lanthanide hydroxides to vary. (Basic strength decreases from La(OH)3 to Lu(OH)3.)

8
Characteristics of transition elements

What are the characteristics of the transition elements and why are they called transition elements? Which of the d-block elements may not be regarded as the transition elements?

Conclusion
Transition elements are elements whose atoms or ions (in a stable oxidation state) have a partially filled d-orbital. These elements lie in the d-block. Their properties are a transition between the s-block and the p-block. That is why they are called transition elements.
Zn, Cd, and Hg are not classified as transition elements because they have a completely filled d-subshell.

In what way is the electronic configuration of the transition elements different from that of the non transition elements?

Conclusion
Transition metals have a partially filled d-orbital. So the electronic configuration of transition elements is (n-1) d1-10 n s0-2.
Non-transition elements either have no d-orbital or have a fully filled d-orbital. So the electronic configuration of non-transition elements is ns1-2 or ns2 n p1-6.

11
Reasons for transition metal properties

Explain giving reasons:

Part (i)

Transition metals and many of their compounds show paramagnetic behaviour.

Conclusion
Transition metals show paramagnetic behaviour.
Paramagnetism happens because of unpaired electrons — each unpaired electron has a magnetic moment from its spin and orbital angular momentum.
But in the first transition series, the orbital angular momentum is quenched.
So the paramagnetism we see comes only from the unpaired electrons.
Part (ii)

The enthalpies of atomisation of the transition metals are high.

Conclusion
Transition elements have a high effective nuclear charge and many valence electrons.
So they form very strong metallic bonds.
This is why the enthalpy of atomisation of transition metals is high.
Part (iii)

The transition metals generally form coloured compounds.

Conclusion
Most complexes of transition metals are coloured. This happens because an electron absorbs radiation from the visible region and jumps from one d-orbital to another.
In the presence of ligands, the d-orbitals split into two sets with different energies. So electrons can move from one set toanother. This transition needs only a small amount of energy, which falls in the visible region.
The metal ion absorbs radiation of one particular wavelength and reflects the rest.
This reflected light gives the solution its colour.
Part (iv)

Transition metals and their many compounds act as good catalyst.

Conclusion
The catalytic activity of transition elements can be explained by two facts.
(a) Because they can show variable oxidation states and form complexes, transition metals form unstable intermediate compounds. This gives the reaction a new path with lower activation energy, Ea.
(b) Transition metals also give the reaction a suitable surface to occur on.

Interstitial compounds in transition metals

What are interstitial compounds? Why are such compounds well known for transition metals?

Conclusion
Transition metals are large and have many interstitial sites.
They can trap small atoms of other elements — such as H, C, and N — in these interstitial sites of their crystal lattice.
The compounds formed this way are called interstitial compounds.

Variable oxidation states comparison

How is the variability in oxidation states of transition metals different from that of the non transition metals? Illustrate with examples.

Conclusion
In transition elements, the oxidation state can vary from +1 up to the highest state, formed by removing all the valence electrons. These oxidation states differ from each other by 1 (Fe2+ and Fe3+ ; Cu+and Cu2+).
In non-transition elements, the oxidation states differ by 2 — for example, +2 and +4 or +3 and +5, etc.

The highest oxidation state is exhibited in oxoanions of a metal.

Conclusion
Oxygen is a strong oxidising agent because of its high electronegativity and small size.
